# Create a bullet list of key macroeconomics and predictors that drive or affect the cryptocurrency market

Here’s a compact list of the big levers that tend to move the crypto market.

- Global interest rate policy (especially the U.S. Federal Reserve’s rate): lower rates and expectations of cuts usually boost liquidity and risk appetite, while hiking cycles like 2022 have coincided with major Bitcoin drawdowns.[^1][^2][^3]
- Inflation and CPI trends: high or rising inflation and currency devaluation can push some investors toward crypto as a potential store of value, but aggressive anti‑inflation hikes often overshadow that and pressure prices.[^4][^3][^1]
- Money supply and global liquidity (e.g., M2, QE/QT): expansions in global money supply and loose liquidity have shown very strong positive correlation with Bitcoin and broader crypto; tight liquidity does the opposite.[^2][^5]
- GDP growth and recession risk: solid GDP growth tends to support risk‑on behavior and capital flowing into both equities and crypto, while recession fears and trade‑war shocks raise risk aversion and can drain demand for volatile assets.[^6][^1][^2]
- Equity market performance and risk sentiment (S\&P 500, Nasdaq, etc.): Bitcoin behaves more like a high‑beta risk asset than a safe haven, with positive and sometimes rising correlation to major stock indices, especially in stress episodes.[^7][^8][^9]
- Market volatility and “fear” gauges: crypto generally performs better in regimes of lower overall market volatility, while spikes in macro/market volatility tend to coincide with risk‑off moves and crypto sell‑offs.[^5][^10]
- U.S. Dollar Index (DXY) and FX conditions: a stronger dollar and rising short‑term yields usually pressure crypto, which often shows a negative correlation with DXY as capital rotates into safer, higher‑yielding dollar assets.[^11][^12][^13][^9][^5]
- Key commodity prices, especially gold and oil: the dollar and gold both act as competing macro hedges; stronger dollar or higher gold prices can weigh on crypto metrics, while oil shocks propagate through inflation and growth expectations.[^13][^9][^7]
- Consumer confidence, leading indicators, and trade data: variables like consumer confidence, leading economic indices, imports, and exports have measurable forecasting power for crypto volatility, especially post‑COVID.[^14][^10]
- Monetary and fiscal policy stance (QE/QT, deficits, stimulus): ultra‑loose policy and fiscal stimulus have historically coincided with crypto bull runs, while tightening and fiscal stress tend to sap speculative flows.[^3][^2][^5]
- Trade policy and geopolitical risk: tariffs, sanctions, and geopolitical tensions can simultaneously raise inflation, recession risk, and currency instability, shifting investors between risk‑off and “outside the system” assets like Bitcoin.[^12][^2][^6][^5]

Key non‑macro predictors that still matter a lot:

- Regulatory and policy announcements (e.g., ETF approvals, bans, tax rules), which can reprice risk and access overnight.[^6][^5]
- Market confidence, adoption, and technology upgrades (institutional adoption, halvings, L2 launches), which change long‑term demand and narratives.[^5][^6]
- Liquidity and capital flows specific to crypto (exchange reserves, fund flows, derivatives positioning), which amplify or dampen macro impulses.[^2][^5]
- Investor sentiment and information flow (news, social media, search trends), which drive speculative cycles and short‑term volatility around macro events.[^14][^6]
